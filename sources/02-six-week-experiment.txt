Yes. I'd turn this into a six-week residential architecture experiment where you're simultaneously learning to design buildings and developing software that helps you design them.

Your proposed workflow is exactly the right direction, with one important adjustment: don't aim to generate renderings that a general contractor could directly build from. Aim to produce a credible architectural design package, backed by a structured building model, with automated checks and clearly identified areas requiring professional engineering.

I would structure the experiment around one question:

Can we go from a real parcel of land and a client's requirements to an intelligently designed, technically evaluated house—with as much of the repetitive work automated as possible?

Here's how I'd approach it.

A Hudson Valley site gives us interesting architectural questions. An NYC residential lot, on the other hand, offers a more accessible public-data environment for initially developing the software. We could use either, but I'd start with one site, one jurisdiction, and one relatively simple building.

## 1. Start with the land, and build a feasibility engine

WEEK 1 · GIS, regulatory research, and environmental constraints

The first thing I'd build is a site dossier: a combination of a map, a regulatory report, and an initial 3D terrain model.

For NYC, we already have excellent public sources:

- [NYC ZoLa](https://zola.planning.nyc.gov/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) — zoning district and land-use research.
- [NYC PLUTO](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Primary-Land-Use-Tax-Lot-Output-PLUTO-/64uk-42ks?utm_source=chatgpt.com) — lot-level property and land-use attributes.
- [USGS 3DEP](https://www.usgs.gov/3d-elevation-program?utm_source=chatgpt.com) — topographic and elevation data.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://zola.planninglabs.nyc\&sz=32)

  NYC’s Zoning & Land Use Map

  +2



We'd collect parcel boundaries, topography, orientation, setbacks, maximum building height, allowed uses, access, and environmental constraints. We'd also identify missing information: for example, utilities, soil-bearing capacity, drainage, and easements.

A GIS parcel boundary is useful for preliminary design, but it's not a replacement for a licensed property survey.

## 2. Learn architecture by developing the brief yourself

WEEK 2 · Spatial planning and schematic design

Before generating any 3D geometry, I'd have you design the spatial relationships.

Start with a structured client program:

```
home:
  target_area_sqft: 1500
  bedrooms: 2
  bathrooms: 2

priorities:
  - south-facing shared living space
  - private bedrooms
  - natural ventilation
  - connection to garden
  - simple construction

constraints:
  - stay within verified buildable area
  - minimize unnecessary circulation
  - preserve important trees
```

Then draw three different floor-plan concepts, initially without worrying about exact dimensions.

The learning exercise is to defend each choice: Why put the kitchen there? Why orient the building this way? What happens to privacy? How far do occupants have to walk? What spaces feel generous versus compressed?

I'd avoid having AI design the first floor plan. Your goal is to develop architectural judgment, not just prompt an image generator.

AI can be useful as a critic, checking your ideas against the brief and identifying relationships you might have overlooked.

## 3. Model the house in 3D

WEEKS 3–4 · Geometry, parametric modeling, and building information

I'd start with Rhino + Grasshopper.

Rhino makes it relatively easy to explore architectural geometry, while Grasshopper lets you program relationships and constraints.

For example, you could define the home using parameters for roof pitch, ceiling height, building depth, glazing ratio, and orientation, then explore alternatives without redrawing everything.

## 4. Automate the analyses and preliminary reviews

WEEK 5 · Analysis engines and design feedback

This is where the software starts to become particularly compelling.

We can build a validation pipeline that runs whenever the model changes.

| Review         | What software could do                                           |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Zoning         | Compare dimensions with verified setback, height, and area rules |
| Solar          | Simulate sunlight, shadowing, and solar exposure                 |
| Environmental  | Estimate energy use and heating/cooling demand                   |
| Space planning | Compare rooms and circulation against the program                |
| Structural     | Flag long spans, unusual cantilevers, missing supports           |
| Cost           | Estimate quantities and preliminary cost ranges                  |
| BIM quality    | Check required building-object attributes and relationships      |

There are already mature engines for some of this. Ladybug Tools performs climate and sunlight studies, while Honeybee connects to Radiance and EnergyPlus for detailed daylight and energy simulation.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.ladybug.tools\&sz=32)

And buildingSMART's [Information Delivery Specification](https://www.buildingsmart.org/standards/bsi-standards/information-delivery-specification-ids/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) provides a standard for automatically checking whether IFC models contain required information.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.buildingsmart.org\&sz=32)

buildingSMART International



The important limitation: we can automate many checks, but not certify engineering safety or legal compliance. Structural design needs verified loads, material specifications, soil conditions, and professional engineering judgment. A model passing automated checks is not an engineering approval.

The goal is to surface mistakes early and produce an organized review package for licensed professionals.

## 5. Generate an architectural package

WEEK 6 · Documentation and presentation

Rather than producing only beautiful renders, the goal is to produce multiple outputs from a shared, dimensionally accurate model.

Our first package would include:

- A site and zoning feasibility report, with assumptions and source citations
- Dimensioned conceptual floor plans, elevations, and building sections
- 3D perspectives and interior renderings
- Solar and energy analysis
- Preliminary material quantities and cost assumptions
- A BIM/IFC model and an automated review report

This would be a compelling schematic design package, not yet a construction set.

To reach the point where a GC can actually build it, we'd need a substantially more developed construction-document package: coordinated foundation and structural plans, engineered connections, building assemblies, waterproofing details, schedules, specifications, building systems, and jurisdiction-specific permit requirements. Licensed professionals would need to perform or verify the required design work.

The distinction matters because beautiful renderings can conceal unresolved or even impossible construction details.

I wouldn't build another CAD application. I'd build an orchestration layer around existing architectural tools.

Project intelligence layer

Model authoring

Given your development background, a practical application stack would be Rails + Postgres/PostGIS for parcels and project data, Python for geometry and simulation workflows, and a Three.js viewer for reviewing models in the browser.

The principle I'd protect is that the structured geometry and building data are the source of truth, not the rendered images or an LLM's interpretation of them.

## What I'd actually build first

Not all six weeks at once. I'd make the first two-week milestone incredibly focused:

An address-to-buildable-envelope prototype.

You choose an address, the application retrieves available parcel information, produces a sourced feasibility report, and displays the known building restrictions as geometry. It distinguishes confirmed facts from assumptions and missing data. You then manually place a simple building volume inside that envelope and receive immediate preliminary feedback.

That first project teaches you land-use research, GIS, 3D geometry, regulatory analysis, and software development. It also gives us something concrete that an architect could review and critique.

From there, the next milestone is to bring your own house design into the system and evaluate two or three genuine architectural alternatives.

I'd be particularly interested in treating the entire house like a product with testable requirements: every major design decision should map back to a client need, a site constraint, or an architectural intention.

One decision to make first: would you rather start with a Hudson Valley landscape, where the architectural design is more compelling, or a Brooklyn/NYC lot, where the public data makes building the first automated feasibility tool more straightforward?
