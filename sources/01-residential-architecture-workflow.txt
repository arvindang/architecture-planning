## 1. Discovery: understanding the client and the land

Usually, architects begin with a client conversation and an initial assessment of the property. These happen before or alongside formal surveying.

Client requirements (called programming):

- How many people will live here? How many bedrooms?
- How do they actually use their home?
- Should the kitchen be the social center?
- What architectural styles or materials appeal to them?
- What's the construction budget?
- What are the priorities and compromises?

Architects translate these conversations into a program: a list of spaces, approximate square footage, desired relationships, and constraints.

Site investigation:

A licensed land surveyor, rather than the architect, typically prepares the formal survey.

This may include property boundaries, elevations, slopes, existing structures, trees, utilities, easements, and other relevant features.

A geotechnical engineer might additionally investigate soil conditions, groundwater, and foundation requirements.

The architect also visits the property, photographs it, observes neighboring buildings, and studies the surrounding environment.

They investigate two categories of constraints:

Legal and regulatory: Zoning, setbacks, maximum height, lot coverage, floor-area restrictions, access, historic preservation, flood zones, and applicable building regulations.

Environmental: Solar orientation, seasonal shadows, wind, rainfall, drainage, prevailing temperatures, topography, existing vegetation, privacy, and views.

For instance, on a residential site in New York, an architect might examine how low winter sunlight could heat a south-facing living room, while designing roof overhangs to reduce summer overheating.

These considerations influence not only window placement, but the building's entire geometry.&#x20;

C. Architecture emerges

Develop floor plans, sections, windows, roof forms, materials, and indoor-outdoor relationships together. The architect repeatedly moves between 2D and 3D.

Imagine a wooded hillside lot.

The architect might start with a rectangular two-story building, then discover that rotating it 20 degrees improves views. Moving the bedrooms upstairs preserves privacy. Splitting the building into two volumes avoids removing a large tree. A courtyard could allow sunlight deeper into the interior.

They'll compare multiple concepts, discuss tradeoffs with the client, and iterate toward an approved schematic design.

## 4. Design development: turning a concept into a real building

Once the client approves a direction, the team starts making the design technically credible.

This includes selecting actual windows and doors, wall assemblies, roof systems, materials, structural arrangements, insulation, heating and cooling strategies, and plumbing locations.

This is also when engineers become deeply involved. The architect coordinates the overall design, while structural engineers design and calculate structural systems, and mechanical, electrical, and plumbing specialists develop their respective systems where required.

These are not all decisions deferred until the end. They inform the design throughout.

## 5. Construction documentation: the technical blueprint

Here's an important distinction: architects increasingly use BIM (Building Information Modeling), rather than producing every drawing independently.

In BIM, a wall isn't just a line. It's an object with thickness, materials, layers, fire-resistance information where needed, and relationships to other building elements.

Changing that wall can update the corresponding plans, sections, elevations, quantities, and schedules.

A construction drawing set can include:

- Dimensioned floor plans
- Elevations and sections
- Foundation and structural drawings

- Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC drawings
- Material and construction specifications

Some calculations and quantities can be extracted from the model, but engineering analysis, code compliance, cost estimation, and documentation still require professional verification.

## 6. Approvals and permitting

There are really two kinds of approval.

Client approvals happen throughout programming, schematic design, and design development.

Government approvals may involve zoning or planning review, design review, and building permit review. The sequence depends on the jurisdiction and property.

Some projects need early zoning approvals before construction documents are finalized. Others can proceed directly to a building permit application.

The building department reviews relevant plans and engineering documents, requests revisions if necessary, and issues permits when the requirements are satisfied.

The architect and engineers provide signed or sealed documents where the law requires them.

## 7. Contractor pricing and construction

A general contractor (GC) can become involved early, particularly when budget or constructability feedback is important. Alternatively, the owner might invite contractors to bid after the construction documents are developed.

The GC typically takes responsibility for organizing construction: subcontractors, labor, procurement, site logistics, schedule, and means and methods.

The architect may remain involved through construction administration: answering RFIs (requests for information), reviewing shop drawings and material submittals, observing construction, reviewing changes, and helping determine whether the work generally conforms to the design documents.

The contractor builds; the architect helps protect the design intent and administers the architect's contract responsibilities.&#x20;

## The bigger insight: architecture isn't a linear handoff

The process is less like design first, calculate second, build third and more like a continuous negotiation between four interconnected things:

Human needs

Program, aesthetics, lifestyle

Site and climate

Geography, sunlight, weather

Technical feasibility

Structure, materials, regulations

Economics

Budget, labor, construction

Iterative architectural design

One of the interesting opportunities in modern architecture software is connecting these traditionally separate workflows. Imagine moving a window in the 3D model and immediately seeing changes to daylight, heat gain, structural implications, material quantities, and estimated construction cost.

Individual tools already do portions of that today, but comprehensive, reliable integration is still a challenge.

The key distinction is that an architect doesn't merely design how a home looks. They coordinate how it functions, interacts with its environment, and can actually be constructed.
